## Intro

A little over two and a half years ago, I joined a young startup in my very first role as a Product Manager.  I recently left this company as part of an org-wide restructuring, but my time there has been an incredible learning experience both personally and professionally. 

Startups are emotionally charged environments and leaving a startup that you care deeply about in a restructuring is an emotional experience. This blog post is part of my process for making sense of what went well and what didn't go well as well as capturing the lessons that I want to take with me as I move forward with my career. 

*NOTE: I am choosing to avoid any identifying information about my prior company or any people involved for this post.*

## What went well

**There is proven market demand for our founding product idea**. Its not always true that there is a market for the product you are trying to build. This was not the case for my prior company where we were building a financial modeling and reporting platform for FP&A teams and their users. What was interesting about our product/market fit challenge was not that there wasn't a ready market who would pay for a service like ours -- everyone we talked to was open to paying for a solution that was better than what they had today -- but they were also always evaluating us against their existing solutions (mostly spreadsheets) and business processes and their switching costs were very high. *More on this later in "What didn't go well"*.
<br><br>

**I learned a TON and made lots of lasting connections**. I took this job with the personal goal of throwing myself into the deepest pool I could find in order to rapidly learn and develop as a product manager. I would argue that I was successful at this goal. During my time at my last company, I was promoted twice and given increasingly more complex and important responsibilities. I always showed up with humility, determination, and empathy even when things were hard. Despite the circumstances of how things ended, I have no doubt that I left a good impression of my ability and who I am as human on everyone that worked with me. I also learned a ton about financial modeling and corporate finance!!! Both as a product manager and as someone who continues to harbor entrepreneurial ambitions, I have no doubt that this education will be invaluable in the future. :muscle: :brain: :muscle:


## What didn't go well

**Revenue growth was difficult**. This is the name of the game in venture backed B2B SaaS. Grow or die :crossed_swords:. There are so, so, so many things that you need to get right and there is normally very little time to correct mistakes. As a young startup, you need to execute extremely well and this proved difficult for us for a variety of reasons. For one, our more established competitors had a huge feature lead and this often led us into a world where we were losing revenue simply because we didn't have `<insert feature name here>`. Compounding this problem were the switching costs that I mentioned previously. Almost every customer had some unique need that we hadn't yet built a solution for even if this one unique problem was only 5% of their overall workflow, they were highly reluctant to paying for a new tool if it didn't solve for everything. 
<br><br>

**We fell into the "peanut butter" trap** :peanuts::peanuts::bread:. What is [peanut buttering](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/brada/looks-like-i-created-a-new-word-peanut-buttering)? Its the tendency to try to evenly distribute resources across a full range of product problems rather than focusing on a few core value propositions where you can meaningfully differentiate. It is the tendency to try to do too much "well enough" at the expense of being truly exceptional in a few areas. While product managers play a role in helping companies to prioritize business and user problems, at startups these fundamental questions of *"What are we going to be exceptional at?*" and conversely *"What are we not going to do?*" are made by the founders. Despite the best intentions of leadership, we engaged in peanut buttering both our GTM motion and product roadmap. This in turn led us to make the most classic of startup blunders. We aligned on a roadmap that was too broad and then over-hired in both R&D and GTM functions to execute on this roadmap. 

## Lessons learned

**The importance of answering "Why Now?"**. One of the unspoken rules of being a product manager is that you must speak in frameworks (just kidding :smile:). Previously, I wrote about a framework that a prior product mentor had shared with me about called the ["What", "Why", and the "Why Now"](https://dlrappeport.com/uncertainty/#the-what-the-why-and-the-why-now). Until now, I don't think I fully appreciated the importance of the "Why Now?" question. Its very easy to fall into the trap of wanting to solve all of our customers' needs right away and its very difficult to have the discipline to truthfully answer the question of *"Why should we solve this user problem now?"* especially if we've already believe that the answers to "What are the user problem and business problem?" and *"Why should we (the company) solve this problem?"* are compelling. After my experience, I now believe that the "Why now?" is actually the most important question for a business to answer and it absolutely cannot be overlooked. Without truthfully examining this question, you will almost certainly find yourself in the peanut butter trap. 
<br><br>

**Building features does not guarantee you've solved user problems**. On reflection, this is my biggest learning and it is going to sound obvious when I say it, but you cannot assume that by building a feature you have actually done anything useful. You must build a solution to a problem and that solution needs to be intuitive (i.e. usable), reliable, and scalable. Too often product roadmapping discussions only involve conversations about features and timelines. This way of thinking can be incredibly dangerous for a few reasons. First, it actually takes a lot of time to go from shipping a software feature to production and then further iterating on it to ensure that you've achieved usability, reliability, and scalability. I have also come to believe that by hyper-focusing on features, we discourage ourselves from moving as quickly as possible and iterating on user problems in an agile manner. Finally, organizations that align themselves around features rather than user problems are at a higher risk of disfunction and system complexity due to [Conway's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway%27s_law); i.e. if we organize and align around features rather than problems, we tend to create systems that appear overly complex and disconnected relative to the problems that they are meant to solve.

## Conclusion

Leaving a job you care deeply about in a restructuring is never easy, but the most important thing for me is that I feel that I've taken the right lessons away from the experience. Having had time to reflect, I feel ready to take on my next challenge and to continue to grow and develop in my product career. If you've read this post, I hope that you find it useful as you navigate your own career in startups or product management.
